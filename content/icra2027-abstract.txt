A controller for a muscle-driven joint runs on a linear model of the muscle, obtained by linearizing a musculoskeletal model at the operating point. At the operating points controllers regulate, Hill-type muscle models with switched activation dynamics have no single linearization. Activation is faster than deactivation, so the activation dynamics switch on the sign of excitation minus activation, and every equilibrium requires the two to be equal. Muscle resists lengthening more stiffly than it drives shortening, so the force–velocity relation switches at zero fibre velocity, and every equilibrium requires zero velocity. An equilibrium therefore has four Jacobians, and the direction of the perturbation picks one. We prove that the two switches satisfy the continuity condition for bimodal piecewise-linear systems, act on disjoint Jacobian entries, and have closed-form slope ratios, then measure what the ambiguity costs. On an elbow model checked against one measured flexion-strength data set, a linearize-and-hold prediction on the wrong branch errs by 14 to 75% of the response to a small activating step at a representative posture and, with both branches wrong, by at least 23% of an activating step at every single-muscle equilibrium tested; three flexors, three parameter sets and an elastic tendon repeat the pattern. In closed-loop model predictive control the cost is transient quality: a central-difference linearization through the kinks overshoots a raising step eight to nine times as much as the naive, sign-test and relinearizing rules, and relinearizing at the measured state cuts the lowering overshoot from 0.37° to 0.02°. Under 50 ms of unmodelled delay the branch rule decides which designs diverge, and co-contraction of an antagonist can cancel the force–velocity kink.
